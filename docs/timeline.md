# Timeline

What happened, in order, with links to the evidence. The narrative is in the [blog](../blog/index.md).

## How the work is done
1. **Back up before any write.** Nothing is written to any flash until a dump exists that was read twice with the
   same hash.
2. **The working second unit is read-only.** It's the reference, not a donor.
3. **Mains.** The power board is never probed while plugged in. Logic runs from a bench supply.
4. **Every claim carries a confidence** (`confirmed` / `likely` / `guess`) **and a source.**
5. **No firmware is distributed**, ever, in any form.

| 5 Nov | The first seven test points measured, main board unpowered: TP1 is on the 5 V supply input; TP2, TP3, TP4, TP7, TP8 and TP9 are ground. [Test points](hardware/testpoints.md). |

That is the shape of the Wi-Fi chip's three boot-mode straps (likely); which chip pin each reaches is the next measurement. [Nets](hardware/nets.md). |
| 5 Nov | Test point TP13, beside the Bluetooth chip, has no connection to the 30-pin connector: the three connector lines that run into that corner end elsewhere. [Test points](hardware/testpoints.md). |

| 5 Nov | Bare pad P9, beside the flash-bus pads, rings to the Wi-Fi chip's JTAG input TDI (pin 16). With the four flash-bus pads it looks like a footprint for a factory programming fixture (guess). [Nets](hardware/nets.md#bare-round-pads-p1-p9). |
| 5 Nov | The other eight bare pads measured to the Wi-Fi chip: four are its flash lines, as traced on 4 Nov, and four are its general-purpose SPI port (GPIO14-17, which can also be a UART or I2C). [Nets](hardware/nets.md#bare-round-pads-p1-p9). |
| 5 Nov | The flash chip's four bus lines measured from end to end: clock, chip select and both data lines run from the Wi-Fi chip through their test pads to the flash pins the datasheet names. [Nets](hardware/nets.md). |
| 5 Nov | The flash runs on the 3.3 V regulator's output, and its HOLD pin is pulled up with about 10 kΩ. So the flash can't be read in circuit with the programmer powering the rail, which would drive the regulator's output backwards; the board gets fed at its power header instead, with the Wi-Fi chip held in reset. [Recovery strategy](firmware/recovery-strategy.md). |
| 5 Nov | The three-hole header J29 beside the Wi-Fi chip: one hole is ground, the other two run to test points TP28 and TP29. The layout of a serial port; which chip pins it reaches is next. [Main board](hardware/main-board.md). |
| 5 Nov | Decision: the flash chip comes off the board for its read-out. The board then never needs power for the backup, and nothing else on it can interfere. It goes back on before the board is ever powered. [Recovery strategy](firmware/recovery-strategy.md). |
| 5 Nov | The Anova Android app, the version (3.6.7) that ran the update, examined: it contains no cooker firmware. It gets a download link from Anova's servers and tells the cooker to fetch the update itself over Wi-Fi. For a Pro, the app's update screen gets no progress number from the cooker, and the 75 % was on the cooker's own display: the cooker's firmware was running and reporting up to that point (`likely`). |
| 5 Nov | Anova's update service, asked the way the app asks, names Pro firmware 3.3.35 as current: a file last changed in January 2022. Two certificate packages the app can send a Pro before the firmware date from 2019. Nothing there changed near the update; which package this unit was installing isn't known. |
| 5 Nov | The Pro firmware package was examined. It is a standard TI update bundle that writes three things at once: the Wi-Fi chip's application, the **whole** Bluetooth chip's flash image, and the Wi-Fi radio service pack. So an interrupted update could have damaged any of the three, not just one. Both chip images in the package are unencrypted and carry the maker's signature; the Bluetooth chip's image leaves its debug port open. This is a by-the-book update bundle; the firmware itself is kept private and never republished. |
| 5 Nov | How the app talks to a Pro, read from the same app version: cooking commands and the cooker's status pass through Anova's cloud; the app never talks to the cooker over the home network. The phone reaches the cooker directly only during setup: over Bluetooth, and over a temporary Wi-Fi access point the cooker opens, where the Wi-Fi chip's built-in setup web server answers (`likely`). How the cooker itself talks to the cloud is not in the app. |
| 5 Nov | The two chips' own firmware was mapped (read-only). They split the work and talk over an internal serial link: one chip is the real-time controller (pump, heater, touch panel, sensors), the other is the Wi-Fi/cloud brain that fetches the cooking job from the cloud, runs the web/update side, drives the lights, and plays the sounds. The start-up beep is the cloud chip's job, so a unit with no beep and no lights points at that chip not running (or no power) rather than the controller — a lead the planned tests can check. |
| 5 Nov | The Wi-Fi chip's own boot loader can read the whole flash out over the three-hole serial header, so the backup can be taken with the flash chip left in place, and an open-source client for that boot loader exists. Desoldering the flash, decided that morning, becomes the fallback. The backup comes first, before the first normal boot, since a normal boot could itself change the flash. Every step is rehearsed first on a TI development board with the same chip family, which arrived today. [Recovery handbook](recovery/index.md). |
| 5 Nov | What a repair must preserve, from the chip maker's documentation: the unit's network address and its cloud identity live on that flash and survive only a file-by-file replacement of the application, not a factory restore or a full reprogramming. So the plan is to replace one file through the boot loader, with the maker's own signed file, and nothing else. [Recovery handbook](recovery/index.md). |
| 5 Nov | The empty switch footprint's three pads are the Wi-Fi chip's boot-mode straps, measured to the chip's pins, and each carries the pull-down resistor the chip maker recommends for its strap: one pad per strap, so the strap that starts its serial boot loader can now be set on its own pad, and the pair that would trigger a factory restore can be kept apart. [Test points](hardware/testpoints.md#j14-strap-pads-x4-x5-x6). |
| 5 Nov | The switch footprint's shared side is fed from the 3.3 V rail through a small resistor, not from the 5 V input, so a closed switch can't put 5 V on the chip. The same reading shows no short on the 3.3 V rail. [Test points](hardware/testpoints.md#j14-strap-pads-x4-x5-x6). |
| 5 Nov | The three-hole header J29's two signal holes each pair with the expected serial-port pin of the Wi-Fi chip, but the resistance reads about 192 kΩ instead of the small series resistor expected. Whether that is a real resistance in the path isn't settled yet, and more readings decide. It matters because a resistance that high would make the header too weak for the fast boot-loader link. [Nets](hardware/nets.md#open). |
| 5 Nov | In diode mode both J29 signal holes show a chip pin's signature (0.7 V one way, 1.5 V the other), so each hole is a live line. A junction that close to the hole doesn't fit a 192 kΩ part between the hole and the chip: either another part sits on the header's side, or the earlier reading landed on the neighbouring pin, a supply pin. A re-check under the microscope decides. [Recovery chapter 1](recovery/01-meter-checks.md#5-diode-mode-on-the-j29-holes). |
| 5 Nov | Decision: the bench sessions start on spring probes. Once the parts are in, the plan is a polarised 2-pin connector on the power header JP5, a 3-pin connector in the serial header J29 and a DIP switch on the boot-mode footprint J14 (the switches arrived today), so a session no longer depends on seven probes staying put. The switch goes on with every position off; the combination that would restore the factory image stays off until the flash is backed up. [Bench kit](hardware/bench-setup.md#bench-kit). |
| 5 Nov | The home router's client records date the stall: the unit's last Wi-Fi traffic was on 28 Oct at 17:52 CET, and the cooker downloads its update itself, so that is when the update stopped (likely). An unknown client named like an Anova device was seen on 4 Nov, while no other Anova device was switched on. Its network address belongs to another maker's range, which a revived Wi-Fi chip would not normally use; whether it was this unit is being checked. [Incident](incident.md). |
| 5 Nov | Rehearsal on a development board with the same Wi-Fi chip: its whole 4 MiB flash was read through the chip's built-in boot loader over a serial line, three times with the same result, without removing anything. The open-source client needed a fix to accept this chip. One session in which the chip's network processor missed the hand-over signal let that processor start its own firmware, which changed two blocks of the flash: the cooker's backup procedure is being changed so that this can't happen before its first copy. |
| 5 Nov | On the development board, the Wi-Fi chip's firmware from the cooker's official update was installed with its original signature, offline, and the chip accepted and started it (likely: its own Wi-Fi access point came up). The same signed image is what a repair of the cooker would reinstall. |
| 5 Nov | Confirmed: the firmware running on the development board is the cooker's own. After booting it had created files of its own on the chip whose names occur only in the cooker's firmware, not in the chip maker's software. |
| 5 Nov | On the development board, the cooker's signed firmware file was also written on its own, through the boot loader, the way the chip maker's own tool writes it: the chip accepted it, raised no security alert, and only that file changed. That is the least destructive repair, but only if the cooker's chip allows it (next line). |
| 6 Nov | The development board, set up in production mode as the cooker's chip probably is: its boot loader still answers and still gives the chip's address, mode and security-alert count, but refuses to read the flash and to read or write single files. So the cooker's flash is backed up with a clip on the flash chip, the Wi-Fi chip held in reset, and a repair comes down to a full image or the chip's own factory restore. |
| 6 Nov | A raw copy of the flash is half readable: ordinary files lie in it as they are, protected ones are encrypted with a key inside the chip. The firmware's own update record is an ordinary file, so the copy of the cooker's flash should show where the update stopped. A parser for the file system was written and checked on the development board. |
| 6 Nov | On the development board, its flash chip was read in circuit, with spring probes on the chip's legs and the Wi-Fi chip held in reset: the read matched the chip's own boot-loader read byte for byte. The method for the cooker's backup is proven on a board where a mistake costs nothing. The reader is a small read-only device of the project's own (an ESP32) that refuses every write command. |
| 6 Nov | The Wi-Fi chip's reset line was traced to a test point (TP33), with a 10 kΩ pull-up and nothing driving it. Held there, the chip never starts, so the flash chip can be read with a clip **in circuit, without desoldering**. The procedure, rehearsed first on the development board, is the [flash backup handbook](flashbackup/index.md). |
| 6 Nov | The cooker's main-board flash was backed up: read in circuit with a clip, the Wi-Fi chip held in reset, two identical reads of all 8 MiB, a copy kept off the bench PC. Its file system is intact. The interrupted update had stored the Bluetooth chip's new image in full and written the Wi-Fi chip's new application image too, but never finished closing that file. The chip's own update safeguard should undo such a half-done update at the next power-up, so the flash alone doesn't explain the dead unit. Next suspect: the Bluetooth chip, which the cooker's firmware updates itself. |
| 6 Nov | Decision: the repair is a full reprogramming of the Wi-Fi chip's flash, with the current firmware (the version the interrupted update was installing) and the unit's own files carried over from the backup: its sounds, its cloud identity and its settings. The image was built offline with the chip maker's tool and checked file by file. Before it is written, the Bluetooth chip's flash is read out, because the new firmware reprograms that chip itself. |
| 6 Nov | The Bluetooth chip's debug port was found on the board, from the microscope mosaic and the chip maker's pinout: its two-wire debug lines come out on test points TP13 and TP14, beside two more for the four-wire variant (`likely`, a meter check confirms). Its flash can be read there with the debug probe built into the development board, read-only, and compared with the two Bluetooth images in the update packages. [Flash backup, page 10](flashbackup/10-cooker-u7.md). |
| 6 Nov | The Bluetooth chip's flash was backed up through its debug port, read twice with the same result. It holds, byte for byte, the Bluetooth firmware the cooker's own flash lists as installed: the interrupted update never reached that chip, so it isn't the cause of the dead unit (likely). With the Wi-Fi chip's flash rolling the half-done update back by itself, neither chip's firmware explains it. Next question: does the Wi-Fi chip start at all. |
| 6 Nov | Found why the cooker is dead: the Wi-Fi chip had locked itself. Its security-alert counter, which the chip maker's security model increments on every failed check, stood at its limit, and at the limit the chip refuses to run until it is reprogrammed. It was reprogrammed through its own boot loader with the current firmware and the unit's own files from the backup; the counter now reads zero. To reach the boot loader, the Bluetooth chip, which shares the serial lines, had to be held in reset. |
| 6 Nov | **The cooker works again.** Reassembled: the buttons and the display respond, a cook can be set and started from the panel, and after a couple of minutes (the new firmware was updating the Bluetooth chip first) the pairing with Wi-Fi and the app worked as it should. A 54 °C test cook ran for ten minutes with the water temperature on point. The power board was fine all along. Nine days from the stalled update to the first cook. [The story](../blog/posts/2026-11-06-it-cooks-again.md). |
| 6 Nov | Two routes for other owners. This unit's Wi-Fi chip turned out to be in development mode, in which its boot loader also reads the flash out, so the whole repair (the status check, the backup, the new image) `likely` runs through the serial header J29 alone: the easy way. A unit in production mode refuses those reads; its backup then needs the clip first, and the new image goes on the same way: the hard way. Not tested yet: the backup through J29 on a locked chip, and whether other units ship in development mode. |
| 6 Nov | Why the security counter ran up. Of the files in the interrupted update only two are "secure" (signature-checked); the interruption left exactly one of them — the new Wi-Fi application image — written but not finalised, so with no signature. The chip verifies that image's signature at every power-up, the check failed each time, and with the unit left plugged in and restarted again and again the counter climbed to its limit and locked. The code that counts the alerts lives in the chip maker's own network-processor firmware, which isn't in any readout, so this is inferred from the update's file list and the stored flash state, not read from that code; a reproduction on the development board would measure it directly. |
| 6 Nov | A closer read of the backup shows the unit never lost its firmware. The flash keeps two copies of each important file so an interrupted update can roll back, and a valid, signed copy of the **previous** application (a different size from the half-written new one) had survived intact, with the support files current — everything needed to start the old firmware. So the dead unit was held down by the security lock alone, not by a missing or broken image: the roll-back safeguard and the anti-tamper lock pulled against each other, and the lock won. It also clears up a common confusion — "secure" here means only a genuine, signed image is allowed to run, not that the image is secret (it downloads in the clear), and it is independent of the debug-door setting the unit happened to ship with. |
| 7 Nov | The update software that handles the install can be studied directly: the routine that writes and commits an update is the chip maker's own library, published in source with their SDK, and the exact SDK version this firmware was built with is on hand. Reading it shows a likely software cause for the lock: an update that **completes but fails its self-test** is cleanly rolled back on the next start, but an update **interrupted part-way** is left in a different, in-between state for which the start-up code has no clean-up step — so the half-written pieces are never rolled back, and repeated restarts of the seemingly-dead unit let the security counter climb to its limit. The backup is consistent with this: it was found in exactly that in-between state. Next: reproduce it on the development board to confirm the trigger, test a small fix (clean up that in-between state on start-up), and offer the finding and fix to Anova — so the aim shifts from only helping owners after a unit dies to helping prevent the brick in the first place. |
| 7 Nov | Decision: nothing gets soldered onto this unit's main board. Spring probes and a clip did the whole repair, so the board stays as built; firmware development, if it ever comes, gets a separate second-hand unit set up as a development station. |
| 7 Nov | Decision: the next goal is our own firmware for the Wi-Fi chip, cloud-free, controlled from home automation over the home network; the Bluetooth chip keeps its firmware and the cooking hardware it drives. It becomes a separate project; the hardware and protocol findings stay documented here. First step: the link between the two chips, captured on the serial header while the cooker runs its own firmware. |

## Corrections
Statements of substance about the cooker that were changed after publication, with the date. Wording and
housekeeping changes aren't listed.

| Date | Correction |
|---|---|
| 2 Nov | "Bricked by the update" was stated as fact. Changed to "died after an interrupted update": the update is the likely cause, not yet a measured one. The measurement that settles it is the first bench job. |
| 3 Nov | Three part readings from the phone photos corrected by the microscope mosaic: U13's second line is `838A` or `B38A` (not `638A`), U2 has 3 leads (not 5), and U6 is an empty footprint (not a fitted 6-lead part). |
| 4 Nov | U17's marking is `59800 / ADJB`, not `S9800 / A0J8`: read against the manufacturer's marking diagram, which identifies the part. |
| 5 Nov | The unknown Anova-named network client of 4 Nov was first called "not this unit" because of its network address. With no other Anova device in the house switched on, that is open again. |
| 6 Nov | The serial header J29 was described (5 Nov) with its two signal holes on the Wi-Fi chip's transmit and receive pins in that order, "through about 192 kΩ". It is the other way round (the first hole is the chip's receive line), the line is driven directly, and the Bluetooth chip shares both lines: the chip maker's boot loader answers through J29 once the Bluetooth chip is held in reset. |
| 6 Nov | The flash-backup line first said the update stopped while writing the Wi-Fi chip's application image "just short of its end", and that "the firmware's own log of the update session survived". A closer look: the image was written in full, only closing the file didn't finish; and the log that survived is from the unit's first setup, not from the interrupted update. |
